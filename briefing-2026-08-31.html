What This Means for You. The MCP agent-identity roadmap and Okta's Agent SSO are now two halves of the same answer, and that changes what an organization can commit to in writing. Every AI rollout eventually reaches the question of how an agent authenticates to your SaaS estate, and for a year the honest answer has been service accounts and long-lived keys. The protocol maintainers have now written down, in a dated primary document, that this is the wrong answer, and named the replacements: DPoP, Workload Identity Federation, ID-JAG and token exchange. ✅ That lets you say something you could not say a week ago: the interim control is a vendor product, the destination is a protocol standard, and the two are not in conflict. ⚠️ The caveat travels with it — the roadmap is a roadmap, and none of DPoP, WIF or ID-JAG is a shipped MCP requirement today. Anyone told otherwise will build a migration plan against a date that does not exist. The second point is the PreModelSwitch and PostModelSwitch hooks, which are a governance primitive hiding in a changelog. If you maintain a documented AI implementation policy that specifies which model handles which class of work, you have until now had no enforcement point for that clause. A hook that can block a model switch is that enforcement point — and it is the difference between a policy that is written and a policy that is operationalized. ✅

  • EU AI Act — standing carry. What This Means for You. The session-cookie campaign is the most useful security conversation available this week, because it defeats the control most organizations believe they have already bought. A stolen session cookie is already authenticated: it does not present a password, so password rotation does not stop it; it does not complete a login, so multi-factor authentication never fires; it does not touch the identity provider, so single sign-on logs show nothing anomalous. ✅ If your AI access-control section says "protected by SSO and 2FA," that is a control statement this campaign walks past without triggering. The corrective belongs in the policy document, not in a training slide: session lifetime limits, revocation procedure, endpoint hygiene requirements for any machine holding an authenticated AI session, and a named owner for the sign-out-versus-clean-up distinction Anthropic drew explicitly. ⚠️ It should not be overstated — this is commodity malware, not an AI-specific attack, and Anthropic said so first. The point is precisely that: the AI account inherits every weakness of the endpoint it is logged in from, and almost no AI policy document currently says so. The second item is the pricing correction, and it is worth building into how your team is taught to check vendor facts. Four separate stories this morning carry the wrong date or the wrong number, and one of them would have led an organization to model a 50% cost increase that is not happening. ✅ The lesson is mechanical rather than moral: for vendor pricing, the vendor's own documentation page is the source, and it took one fetch.
