Yesterday's two calendar events landed, and the tape broke hard. The combination produced the worst equity session since April 2025 — and then, after the close, the AI-capex earnings the market had been waiting on arrived: Microsoft beat on revenue but guided next year's capex sharply higher, and Meta beat on revenue but missed on earnings and fell about 10% after hours. The one item that most affects anyone building agents did not move: the MCP 2026-07-28 stateless specification is now the standing protocol and is rolling into Claude. 1. AI Market Trends

Wednesday was a broad risk-off rout — the worst session since April 2025. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 1,153.18 points, or 2.19%, to 51,594.14; the S&P 500 lost 1.52% to 7,316; and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 1.74% to 24,443 as a hawkish Fed hold, rising yields and a collapsed Middle East ceasefire hit stocks together (Source: BBN Times, "DJIA Plunges to 51,594.14," July 29; Yahoo Finance, "Dow plunges by 1,100 points," July 29; The Washington Post, "How major US stock indexes fared 7/29/2026" — press). ✅ These are settled US closes.

The chip selloff deepened into bear-market territory — and the driver is a repricing of AI capex, not a demand scare. Semiconductor stocks have now fallen more than 20% from their highs, shedding more than $1 trillion in market value over the stretch, after overnight selling in Asia hit Samsung, SK Hynix and SoftBank. On Wednesday Micron fell nearly 10%, ASML about 5.2% on the report that a Chinese firm is building an immersion deep-ultraviolet (DUV) lithography machine, TSMC about 3.5%, Broadcom about 1.7%, and Nvidia about 1.1% (Source: CNBC, "Chip stocks shed more than $1 trillion," July 29; TipRanks, July 29 — press). Analysts framed it as "less about weakening AI demand and more about a repricing of expectations after an exceptionally strong rally." ⚠️ The China DUV report remains single-thread (The Information) and not independently confirmed; treat the causation as reported, the price moves as settled.

The after-close earnings sharpened the "who pays for the compute" question rather than settling it. Microsoft beat — fiscal-Q4 revenue of $90.01 billion, up about 18%, against an $87.62 billion consensus — but guided FY2027 capital expenditure to $255–260 billion, well above the roughly $190 billion spent in fiscal 2026, a step-up that overshadowed a strong Azure print (Source: TradingKey, "Microsoft Q4 FY2026," July 29; Money Morning, July 30 — press). Meta beat on the top line — revenue $60.8 billion, up about 28% — but missed on earnings, with diluted EPS of $6.18 against a $7.23 consensus and net income of $15.8 billion, down from $18.34 billion a year ago, as total expenses rose 55% (including $2.4 billion in legal charges and $1.18 billion in severance); the stock fell about 9.6% after hours (Source: GuruFocus, July 29; TechTimes, "Meta Q2 Beats Revenue," July 29 — press). ✅ These are reported results.

Private capital keeps clearing at the open-weight and vertical-agent layer even as the public tape reprices. Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 open weights (out July 26) reinforce a shareholder push for a Hong Kong listing, and the model has landed as the largest publicly released model to date (Source: QZ; TrendingTopics, July 26 — press; ⚠️ listing not company-confirmed). Money continues to arrive above the compute layer even as equipment and memory names sell off underneath.

Divergence Analysis

The three lenses agree on the deployable core and split, again, on what is moving it. Relevance stays topped by MCP itself: the stateless spec is standing, the Enterprise-Managed Authorization extension is stable, downloads have passed 400M a month, and support is rolling into Claude — a dated migration task, not a preview. Momentum swung hard onto the macro-and-earnings verdict: the Fed's hawkish split, an Iran shock, a chip bear market, and two capex-heavy earnings reports that beat on revenue but raised the "who pays" question. Maturity barely moved — Opus 4.8, GPT-5.6 and Sonnet 5 remain the proven set, Kimi K3 the assessable open challenger, and Gemini 3.5 Pro still not assessable at all. The tension of the day is the sharpest version yet of a familiar one: the item ranked #1 on Relevance (a shipped, stable engineering standard now entering Claude) pulls toward cheaper, more durable agents, while the item ranked #1 on Momentum (an AI-capex cycle the market just repriced downward) pulls toward doubt about the economics of the compute those agents run on.

What This Means for You. For organizations adopting AI, a market that just repriced AI capex downward is not a reason to change what you deploy — it is the argument for how you deploy it. The software did not move on Wednesday's tape: proven flagship models behind MCP connectors are exactly the durable, lower-cost stack a capex-skeptical market rewards, and the now-standing spec is meant to make that layer cheaper to scale. The concrete action for any team running an AI readiness review is unchanged and dated: exercise every connector against the beta SDKs, confirm nothing depends on the removed handshake or session header, and note that the Enterprise-Managed Authorization extension is now stable for anything touching sensitive data. The Fed-and-Iran volatility is context for situational awareness, not a change to what you can deploy. An open-weight model like Kimi K3 stays a live option for data-residency-sensitive work, and Gemini's continued absence keeps reinforcing a simple model-selection discipline: "announced" and "assessable" are different states, and a selection policy should encode the difference.

The immediate task is to run every connector against the beta Python/TypeScript/Go/C# SDKs and confirm nothing relies on the removed handshake or session header; the stable enterprise-authorization extension is the piece to flag for compliance-sensitive work, since it changes how single-login access is centrally governed.
