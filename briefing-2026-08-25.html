Divergence Analysis

Relevance moved because the vendor surface finally moved, and the shape of the release is the story. Sixty changes is not a maintenance drop, and the ones that matter are not agent capabilities — they are cost accounting, identity, and the visibility of managed settings. modelPricing means an organization can stop reconciling /cost against an invoice. Keyless Console sign-in removes the standard objection from security teams that forbid long-lived API keys. The Skipped sources line in /status answers the single most common managed-deployment question — why is my policy file not taking effect. These are the changes you make when your users are organizations rather than individuals.

The MCP roadmap is the item this series should have had yesterday, and progressive discovery is why. The maintainers have written down, in plain language, that a hundred-tool server is a design error: the model pays for the whole surface before the user asks anything, and selection quality degrades as the list grows. Anyone assembling a workspace out of many connectors has been feeling that without a name for it. There is now a name and a stated direction, and it argues for building narrow, task-scoped servers today rather than waiting for the protocol to rescue a wide one.

Momentum has stopped being about models and become about ownership and cost. Three of the five entries in that lens are corporate-development stories — a hub exploring a sale, an answer engine being repriced by its chip supplier, a memory complex selling off on the price of the thing everyone needs. None of them is a capability event. Maturity, meanwhile, did not move at all: no new model in three days, the newest entrant's date disputed between two trackers, and Gemini 3.5 Pro at ninety-eight days.

Put together: the software got more governable, the protocol admitted a design problem, and the market spent the day arguing about what compute costs. Two of those three came from documents published by the people who build the thing. The third came from four incompatible explanations of the same sell-off.

What This Means for You. Three items, and the first is unusually concrete. modelPricing and the keyless Console sign-in are both directly usable this week. Nearly every managed deployment eventually produces the same two frictions: a finance conversation where the tool's cost display does not match the contract, and a security conversation where the organization's policy forbids the API keys the setup assumed. Both now have a first-party answer, and both belong in the standard setup checklist rather than being discovered three months in. The Skipped sources line in /status deserves its own line in the runbook — it converts the most frustrating class of managed-settings failure from a mystery into a readable diagnostic. Second, the MCP roadmap should change how you scope connector work. The maintainers have now said in writing that wide servers degrade tool selection and cost tokens before the first question — an argument many practitioners have been making from experience, and one that is now citable. Practically, it favours several narrow, task-scoped MCP servers over one broad one — a design choice that is cheap to make now and expensive to unwind. Third, the reliability fixes matter more than they read. A remote MCP server that never recovers from a dropped connection in a headless session is exactly the failure mode of a scheduled overnight job. If you run those, upgrading is not optional maintenance this week.
